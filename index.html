Treat every clue as if it were independent. It's usually false, and it works anyway.

A naive Bayes classifier asks, for each class, "how likely is each feature on its own?" and multiplies the answers. Fitting it is just counting. It turns out to be a close cousin of logistic regression, and it's the standard example of a generative classifier, one that models how the data were made rather than only where the boundary goes. §9.3

The naive Bayes assumption: score each clue on its own, then multiply

A generative classifier learns two things: how common each class is, \(p(y=c)\), and what inputs from each class look like, \(p(\mathbf x\mid y=c)\). Bayes' rule then flips them into \(p(y=c\mid\mathbf x)\). The hard part is \(p(\mathbf x\mid y)\) when \(\mathbf x\) has many features. Naive Bayes makes it easy by assuming the features are independent once you know the class.

Explain itAt your level

You're a detective sorting letters into two boxes: junk and real letters from friends.

You've learned some clues. The word "FREE" shows up in most junk letters but hardly ever in letters from friends. The word "lunch" is the other way around.

When a new letter arrives, you look at each clue one at a time and ask: "Does this clue point toward junk or toward friends? How strongly?" Then you add up all the hints.

The "naive" part: you pretend the clues have nothing to do with each other. In real life, "FREE" and "money" often show up together, so you might count the same hint twice. Detectives who do this are a bit too sure of themselves. They still pick the right box most of the time.

Suppose 5 of your 12 saved emails are spam. Before reading a new email, the chance it's spam is 5/12. That's the prior.

Now the email contains "free". In spam, "free" shows up in about 4 out of 5 emails. In normal email, about 1 out of 7. Bayes' rule says: multiply the prior by how likely that word is in each class, then rescale so the two answers add to 1.

With several words, naive Bayes just keeps multiplying: prior × P(word 1 | class) × P(word 2 | class) × … It treats the words like independent coin flips within each class. That's the naive Bayes assumption.

Why not track how words go together? With 6 words there are \(2^6 = 64\) possible on/off patterns per class, and with 10,000 words there are more patterns than atoms in the universe. Naive Bayes needs one number per word per class.

The assumption is conditional independence given the class (Eq. 9.46):

\(p(\mathbf x\mid y=c,\boldsymbol\theta)=\prod_{d=1}^D p(x_d\mid y=c,\theta_{dc})\).

Plug into Bayes' rule and you get Eq. 9.47. The denominator is the same sum over classes for every \(c\), so for classification you only need the numerators. In practice you work in logs: \(\log\pi_c+\sum_d\log p(x_d\mid\theta_{dc})\). Each feature adds its own vote.

Note what's not assumed: features need not be independent marginally. "free" and "winner" are strongly correlated overall, because both are common in spam. NB only says that within spam, knowing one tells you nothing extra about the other.

Parameter count: \(O(CD)\), against \(O(C\,2^D)\) for a full joint over binary features. Few parameters means low variance and little overfitting, which is a big reason NB works well even when the assumption is wrong.

NB is a generative model with a fully factorized class-conditional, i.e. the graphical model \(y\to x_d\) for every \(d\) with no edges among the \(x_d\). The resulting posterior log-odds are additive in per-feature terms: \(\log\frac{p(y=1\mid\mathbf x)}{p(y=0\mid\mathbf x)}=\log\frac{\pi_1}{\pi_0}+\sum_d\log\frac{p(x_d\mid 1)}{p(x_d\mid 0)}\). That is a generalized additive model, and linear in sufficient statistics for exponential-family features (§9.3.4).

  • Why it works when wrong (Domingos & Pazzani 1997): 0-1 loss only needs the argmax to be right. Correlated evidence inflates the log-odds magnitude but often preserves its sign, so NB's ranking survives while its probabilities are badly miscalibrated.
  • Bias–variance: the \(O(CD)\) parameterization has high bias but very low variance. Ng & Jordan (2002) show NB approaches its (worse) asymptotic error with \(O(\log D)\) examples, versus \(O(D)\) for logistic regression.
  • Practice: multinomial NB on TF-IDF counts is still a strong text baseline, especially with complement NB (Rennie et al. 2003) for class imbalance.

Math, slowlyEquations 9.46 – 9.47

Start from Bayes' rule

\[ p(y=c\mid\mathbf x,\boldsymbol\theta)=\frac{p(y=c\mid\boldsymbol\pi)\;p(\mathbf x\mid y=c,\boldsymbol\theta)}{\sum_{c'}p(y=c'\mid\boldsymbol\pi)\;p(\mathbf x\mid y=c',\boldsymbol\theta)} \]

The numerator is "prior × class-conditional density." The denominator just makes the answers add to 1.

Make the naive assumption

\[ p(\mathbf x\mid y=c,\boldsymbol\theta)=\prod_{d=1}^{D}p(x_d\mid y=c,\theta_{dc}) \tag{9.46} \]
  1. The exact chain rule is \(p(x_1,\dots,x_D\mid c)=p(x_1\mid c)\,p(x_2\mid x_1,c)\,p(x_3\mid x_1,x_2,c)\cdots\). Always true, but each factor depends on everything before it, so it needs a huge table.
  2. Naive Bayes drops every "given the other features" part: \(p(x_d\mid x_{1:d-1},c)\approx p(x_d\mid c)\). This is the assumption. Once you know the class, the other features don't change your belief about \(x_d\).
  3. Each factor has its own small parameter \(\theta_{dc}\), one per feature per class. For a binary word, \(\theta_{dc}\) is the chance that word \(d\) appears in an email of class \(c\).

Put it together

\[ p(y=c\mid\mathbf x,\boldsymbol\theta)=\frac{\pi_c\prod_{d=1}^{D}p(x_d\mid y=c,\theta_{dc})}{\sum_{c'}\pi_{c'}\prod_{d=1}^{D}p(x_d\mid y=c',\theta_{dc'})} \tag{9.47} \]

In logs the product becomes a sum, which is what the ledger in the Play box shows (here for two classes):

\[ \log\frac{p(\class{ts}{\text{spam}}\mid\mathbf x)}{p(\class{th}{\text{ham}}\mid\mathbf x)}=\underbrace{\log\frac{\pi_{\text{spam}}}{\pi_{\text{ham}}}}_{\text{prior vote}}+\sum_{d=1}^D\underbrace{\log\frac{p(x_d\mid\class{ts}{\text{spam}})}{p(x_d\mid\class{th}{\text{ham}})}}_{\text{word }d\text{'s vote}} \]

Why "only \(O(CD)\) parameters" matters

With \(D\) binary features, a full table of \(p(\mathbf x\mid c)\) needs \(2^D-1\) numbers per class. Naive Bayes needs \(D\).

§9.3.1

Example models: pick a one-feature distribution to match each feature type

Eq. 9.46 leaves open what each \(p(x_d\mid y=c,\theta_{dc})\) looks like. You pick the simplest distribution that fits the feature: Bernoulli for on/off, categorical for one-of-K, Gaussian for real numbers. You can even mix types in one model.

Explain itAt your level

Different clues need different kinds of notes.

For a yes/no clue like "is this square of the picture colored in?", you write down how often it's yes for each kind of thing. For a pick-one clue like "what color is the fruit?", you write how often each color shows up. For a measuring clue like "how long is it?", you write down the usual size and how much it wiggles around.

To read a handwritten number, the computer looks at every little square. It has learned "for a 1, this middle column is usually colored." It checks each square, one by one, and picks the number that fits best.

  • Bernoulli (yes/no): one number per feature per class, the chance it's "on." In the digits demo, each pixel of each digit class has its own chance of being black. Those chances, drawn as pictures, look like blurry digits (Figure 9.6).
  • Categorical (one of K choices): K numbers per feature per class that add to 1, like "apples are red 70%, green 25%, yellow 5%."
  • Gaussian (real number): a bell curve per feature per class, with a mean and a spread. Because NB treats features separately, each class's bell-shaped "cloud" in 2-D is lined up with the axes: it can be wide or tall, but never tilted.
  • Bernoulli NB: \(p(\mathbf x\mid c)=\prod_d\theta_{dc}^{x_d}(1-\theta_{dc})^{1-x_d}\). On binarized MNIST this gets 84.3% test accuracy with no notion that neighboring pixels are related. The 7×9 mini-digits here behave the same way.
  • Categorical NB: \(p(\mathbf x\mid c)=\prod_d\prod_k\theta_{dck}^{\mathbb I(x_d=k)}\).
  • Gaussian NB: \(p(\mathbf x\mid c)=\prod_d\mathcal N(x_d\mid\mu_{dc},\sigma^2_{dc})=\mathcal N(\mathbf x\mid\boldsymbol\mu_c,\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol\sigma^2_c))\). This is GDA/QDA with diagonal covariance. Boundaries are still quadratic, but the class ellipses are axis-aligned. Turn up the correlation in the Gaussian tab to see where that hurts.
  • Any per-feature exponential family works: Poisson for counts, multinomial for bag-of-words (the "multinomial event model," McCallum & Nigam 1998, usually beats multivariate Bernoulli on long documents), von Mises for angles, KDE for nonparametric features ("flexible Bayes").
  • Bernoulli NB scores absent words (\(1-\theta\)); multinomial NB ignores them. With big vocabularies the Bernoulli event model is dominated by thousands of absent-word factors.
  • Gaussian NB versus GDA: both are \(O(CD)\) for means, but diagonal \(\Sigma_c\) costs \(O(CD)\) instead of \(O(CD^2)\), and it's always invertible. It's the natural shrinkage endpoint of regularized discriminant analysis (Friedman 1989).
  • Relaxations: TAN (tree-augmented NB), AODE, and semi-naive Bayes add a few dependencies back for a modest variance cost.

Math, slowlyThe three class-conditional families

Binary features: multivariate Bernoulli NB

\[ p(\mathbf x\mid y=c,\boldsymbol\theta)=\prod_{d=1}^D\mathrm{Ber}(x_d\mid\theta_{dc})=\prod_{d=1}^D\theta_{dc}^{\,x_d}\,(1-\theta_{dc})^{1-x_d} \]

The exponent trick: if \(x_d=1\) the factor is \(\theta_{dc}\); if \(x_d=0\) it's \(1-\theta_{dc}\). Taking logs,

\[ \log p(\mathbf x\mid c)=\sum_d\big[x_d\log\theta_{dc}+(1-x_d)\log(1-\theta_{dc})\big] \]

The digits demo sums this over all 63 pixels for each class. The "why" picture shows, pixel by pixel, the difference of this sum's terms between the top two classes.

Categorical features

\[ p(\mathbf x\mid y=c,\boldsymbol\theta)=\prod_{d=1}^D\mathrm{Cat}(x_d\mid\boldsymbol\theta_{dc})=\prod_{d=1}^D\prod_{k=1}^K\theta_{dck}^{\,\mathbb I(x_d=k)} \]

The indicator \(\mathbb I(x_d=k)\) is 1 for the value you saw and 0 for the others, so it picks out one entry per feature. Bernoulli is the \(K=2\) case.

Real-valued features: Gaussian NB

\[ p(\mathbf x\mid y=c,\boldsymbol\theta)=\prod_{d=1}^D\mathcal N(x_d\mid\mu_{dc},\sigma^2_{dc})=\mathcal N\!\big(\mathbf x\,\big|\,\boldsymbol\mu_c,\;\operatorname{diag}(\sigma^2_{1c},\dots,\sigma^2_{Dc})\big) \]
  1. A multivariate Gaussian with a diagonal covariance has zero correlation between coordinates. For Gaussians, zero correlation means independence, so the density factors into 1-D bells.
  2. So Gaussian NB = Gaussian discriminant analysis (§9.2) restricted to diagonal \(\Sigma_c\). Each class's ellipse can stretch along \(x_1\) or \(x_2\) but can't rotate.
  3. Different \(\sigma^2_{dc}\) per class give a quadratic boundary; equal ones give a linear one. The \(x_d^2\) terms cancel only when the variances match (see §9.3.4).

§9.3.2

Model fitting: the maximum likelihood estimate is just counting

The log-likelihood splits into one piece for the class prior and one separate piece for each (feature, class) pair. Each piece has a one-line closed-form answer. No gradient descent, no iterations: count, divide, done.

Explain itAt your level

How does the detective learn the clues? By counting.

Take all the junk letters you've saved. In how many does "FREE" appear? If it's 4 out of 5, write down "FREE: 4 out of 5 junk letters." Do the same for every word, and then do the same for the letters from friends.

That's all the "learning" there is. No guessing and checking, just tally marks.

Every number in the model is a fraction you can count off the table:

  • Prior: (number of spam emails) ÷ (all emails). 5 ÷ 12 ≈ 0.42.
  • Word chance: (spam emails containing "free") ÷ (spam emails). 4 ÷ 5 = 0.8.
  • Gaussian feature: the average of that feature over a class's examples, and the average squared distance from that average (the variance).

Watch out for zeros: "meeting" never appears in our spam, so the count says the chance is 0 ÷ 5 = 0. One "meeting" in a spam email would then make spam impossible, no matter what else it says. §9.3.3 fixes that.

Writing the likelihood with indicators \(\mathbb I(y_n=c)\) lets the log turn the double product into a double sum that decouples (Eq. 9.50–9.51): one term depends only on \(\boldsymbol\pi\), and each of the \(CD\) terms depends only on its own \(\theta_{dc}\) and only on the data \(\mathcal D_{dc}=\{x_{nd}:y_n=c\}\).

So you maximize each term separately, and each is a textbook MLE: \(\hat\pi_c=N_c/N\) (categorical, with a Lagrange multiplier for \(\sum_c\pi_c=1\)); \(\hat\theta_{dck}=N_{dck}/N_c\); \(\hat\theta_{dc}=N_{dc}/N_c\) for Bernoulli; sample mean and (biased, divide-by-\(N_c\)) variance for Gaussian.

Cost: one pass through the data, \(O(ND)\).

The decomposition is a consequence of the joint likelihood factoring over a directed graphical model with fully observed nodes: each CPD's parameters are global-independent, so the MLE is a set of local MLEs given sufficient statistics \(\{N_c, N_{dck}\}\) (or \(\sum x, \sum x^2\) per class).

  • Streaming / distributed: sufficient statistics are additive, so training is a map-reduce over counts, and online updates are exact.
  • Degeneracies: \(\hat\theta=0\) or \(1\) gives \(\log 0\); \(\hat\sigma^2_{dc}=0\) for a constant feature gives infinite density. Practical fixes: additive smoothing (§9.3.3) and a variance floor (scikit-learn adds \(\epsilon\cdot\max_d\operatorname{Var}(x_d)\), with \(\epsilon=10^{-9}\)).
  • The MLE for \(\sigma^2\) divides by \(N_c\), not \(N_c-1\): it's biased, but that's the maximizer.

Math, slowlyEquations 9.48 – 9.55

Write down the likelihood

  1. \(\displaystyle p(\mathcal D\mid\boldsymbol\theta)=\prod_{n=1}^N\Big[\mathrm{Cat}(y_n\mid\boldsymbol\pi)\prod_{d=1}^D p(x_{nd}\mid y_n,\boldsymbol\theta_d)\Big]\) (9.48) Examples are i.i.d.; each one is "label prob × feature probs given the label."
  2. \(\displaystyle =\prod_{n=1}^N\Big[\mathrm{Cat}(y_n\mid\boldsymbol\pi)\prod_{d=1}^D\prod_{c=1}^C p(x_{nd}\mid\theta_{dc})^{\mathbb I(y_n=c)}\Big]\) (9.49) The exponent \(\mathbb I(y_n=c)\) is 1 only for the true class, so the product over \(c\) just picks the right \(\theta_{dc}\).
  3. \(\displaystyle \log p(\mathcal D\mid\boldsymbol\theta)=\Big[\sum_{n}\sum_{c}\mathbb I(y_n=c)\log\pi_c\Big]+\sum_{c}\sum_{d}\Big[\sum_{n:\,y_n=c}\log p(x_{nd}\mid\theta_{dc})\Big]\) (9.50) Log of a product is a sum of logs; regroup the terms by \((d,c)\).
  4. \(\displaystyle =\log p(\mathcal D_y\mid\boldsymbol\pi)+\sum_c\sum_d\log p(\mathcal D_{dc}\mid\theta_{dc})\) (9.51) \(1 + CD\) separate terms. Each parameter shows up in exactly one, so you can maximize them one at a time.

Solve each piece

\[ \hat\pi_c=\frac{N_c}{N} \qquad\qquad \hat\theta_{dck}=\frac{N_{dck}}{\sum_{k'}N_{dck'}}=\frac{N_{dck}}{N_c} \tag{9.52} \]

where \(N_{dck}=\sum_n\mathbb I(x_{nd}=k,\,y_n=c)\) counts how often feature \(d\) took value \(k\) among class-\(c\) examples. For binary features this becomes

\[ \hat\theta_{dc}=\frac{N_{dc}}{N_c}\qquad\text{(fraction of class-}c\text{ examples where feature }d\text{ is on)} \tag{9.53} \]

and for Gaussian features, the class-wise sample mean and variance:

\[ \hat\mu_{dc}=\frac{1}{N_c}\sum_{n:\,y_n=c}x_{nd}\qquad \hat\sigma^2_{dc}=\frac{1}{N_c}\sum_{n:\,y_n=c}(x_{nd}-\hat\mu_{dc})^2 \tag{9.54–9.55} \]
Where 9.53 comes from: the class-\(c\) term for feature \(d\) is \(N_{dc}\log\theta+(N_c-N_{dc})\log(1-\theta)\). Set its derivative \(\frac{N_{dc}}{\theta}-\frac{N_c-N_{dc}}{1-\theta}\) to zero and solve: \(\theta=N_{dc}/N_c\).

§9.3.3

Bayesian naive Bayes: add pseudo-counts so nothing is ever impossible

Put a Dirichlet (for two outcomes, a Beta) prior on every \(\boldsymbol\theta_{dc}\) and on \(\boldsymbol\pi\). Because the prior is conjugate, the posterior is the same kind of distribution with the counts added on. Predicting with the posterior mean is the same as adding "pseudo-counts" \(\breve\beta\) to every count. With \(\breve\beta=1\) that's add-one (Laplace) smoothing.

Explain itAt your level

You've never seen a junk letter that says "meeting." Does that mean a junk letter can never say "meeting"?

Of course not. You've only seen 5 junk letters! So before you start counting, you pretend you've already seen one junk letter with every word and one without it. Those pretend letters keep any count from being exactly zero.

With lots of real letters, the pretend ones barely matter. With only a few, they keep you from being silly.

The fix: add 1 to the "yes" count and 1 to the "no" count before dividing.

"meeting" in spam: instead of \(0/5=0\), use \((0+1)/(5+2)=1/7\approx0.14\). "free" in spam: instead of \(4/5=0.8\), use \((4+1)/(5+2)=5/7\approx0.71\).

Every estimate gets pulled a bit toward 1/2. The pull is strong when there's little data and weak when there's lots. Add 0 and you're back to plain counting. Add a lot, and every estimate is close to 1/2 regardless of the data.

Prior \(\theta_{dc}\sim\mathrm{Dir}(\breve{\boldsymbol\beta}_{dc})\), likelihood categorical, so the posterior is \(\mathrm{Dir}(\breve{\boldsymbol\beta}_{dc}+\mathbf N_{dc})\) (Eq. 9.56). The posterior predictive for a new feature value is the posterior mean (Eq. 9.57):

\(p(x_d=k\mid y=c,\mathcal D)=\dfrac{\breve\beta_{dck}+N_{dck}}{\sum_{k'}(\breve\beta_{dck'}+N_{dck'})}\).

Because each \(\theta_{dc}\) appears in its own factor, integrating them out factorizes too, and the full Bayesian prediction is "plug in the posterior means" (Eq. 9.59). That's unusual: in most models, plugging in a point estimate is only an approximation.

\(\breve\beta=0\): MLE. \(\breve\beta=1\): Laplace smoothing (uniform prior). \(\breve\beta=\tfrac12\): Jeffreys prior.

  • The posterior predictive is exact here because the predictive for one new \(\mathbf x\) is linear in each \(\theta_{dck}\) (a product of distinct, a-posteriori independent parameters), so \(\mathbb E[\prod\theta]=\prod\mathbb E[\theta]\). This fails for predicting several new points jointly, where repeated use of the same \(\theta\) gives Dirichlet-multinomial terms.
  • Additive smoothing is a shrinkage estimator toward the prior mean, with weight \(\sum_k\breve\beta_k/(N_c+\sum_k\breve\beta_k)\). Tune \(\breve\beta\) (scikit-learn's alpha) by cross-validation or empirical Bayes (maximize the Dirichlet-multinomial marginal likelihood).
  • For text, Laplace smoothing over a 50k-word vocabulary hands a lot of mass to unseen words. Lidstone (\(\breve\beta\ll1\)), Good–Turing, or hierarchical priors that share strength across classes do better.
  • For Gaussian features the analogous conjugate prior is Normal–Inverse-Gamma per \((d,c)\); the posterior predictive is a Student-t, not a plug-in Gaussian.

Math, slowlyEquations 9.56 – 9.59

Conjugate prior in, same family out

\[ p(\boldsymbol\theta\mid\mathcal D)=\mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol\pi\mid\widehat{\boldsymbol\alpha})\prod_{d=1}^D\prod_{c=1}^C\mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol\theta_{dc}\mid\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}_{dc}),\qquad \widehat\alpha_c=\breve\alpha_c+N_c,\quad \widehat\beta_{dck}=\breve\beta_{dck}+N_{dck} \tag{9.56} \]

(\(\breve{\ }\) marks the prior's values and \(\widehat{\ }\) the posterior's.) The Dirichlet density is \(\propto\prod_k\theta_k^{\beta_k-1}\) and the likelihood is \(\propto\prod_k\theta_k^{N_k}\). Multiply them and the exponents add, so the posterior is Dirichlet with \(\beta_k+N_k\). That's why \(\breve\beta\) acts like counts from "prior data."

Predict with the posterior mean

\[ p(x_d=k\mid y=c,\mathcal D)=\bar\theta_{dck}=\frac{\widehat\beta_{dck}}{\sum_{k'}\widehat\beta_{dck'}}=\frac{\breve\beta_{dck}+N_{dck}}{\sum_{k'}\big(\breve\beta_{dck'}+N_{dck'}\big)} \tag{9.57} \]
  1. \(\breve\beta_{dck}=0\) gives \(N_{dck}/N_c\), the MLE of Eq. 9.52. No prior data: just count.
  2. \(\breve\beta_{dck}=1\) is add-one (Laplace) smoothing. For binary features: the denominator gains 2 because there are two outcomes.
    \[ \bar\theta_{dc}=\frac{\breve\beta_{dc1}+N_{dc1}}{\breve\beta_{dc0}+N_{dc0}+\breve\beta_{dc1}+N_{dc1}}=\frac{1+N_{dc1}}{2+N_{dc}} \tag{9.58} \]
    (Here \(N_{dc}\) means \(N_c\), the total class-\(c\) count, as in the book.)
  3. With lots of data, \(N\gg\breve\beta\), so \(\bar\theta\to\) the MLE. The prior washes out.

Predict the label

\[ p(y=c\mid\mathbf x,\mathcal D)\;\propto\;p(y=c\mid\mathcal D)\prod_d p(x_d\mid y=c,\mathcal D)=\bar\pi_c\prod_d\prod_k\bar\theta_{dck}^{\,\mathbb I(x_d=k)},\qquad \bar\pi_c=\frac{\widehat\alpha_c}{\sum_{c'}\widehat\alpha_{c'}} \tag{9.59} \]
The zero-count trap in numbers: with \(\breve\beta=0\), "winner" has \(\hat\theta_{\text{ham}}=0\) and "meeting" has \(\hat\theta_{\text{spam}}=0\). An email with both makes both class scores exactly 0, and Eq. 9.47 becomes \(0/0\). Any \(\breve\beta>0\) fixes it. Set the slider to 0 to see it.

§9.3.4

Naive Bayes and logistic regression have the same shape of answer

Take NB's posterior, write it in logs, and it turns into a softmax of a linear function of the (one-hot) features. That's exactly the form of multinomial logistic regression. The two models can represent the same set of classifiers. They differ in how they choose the weights: NB maximizes the joint likelihood \(p(\mathbf x,y)\), logistic regression the conditional likelihood \(p(y\mid\mathbf x)\).

Explain itAt your level

Every clue gives the detective a number of points toward junk or toward friends.

"FREE" might be worth +2 points toward junk. "lunch" might be worth −1. Add up the points and see which side wins.

There are two ways to decide how many points each clue is worth. Naive Bayes counts how often each clue shows up in each box. Another method, called logistic regression, keeps adjusting the points until it sorts the practice letters as well as it can. Same kind of scorecard, two different ways of filling it in.

Naive Bayes multiplies factors. Logs turn multiplying into adding. So "log of the spam score minus log of the ham score" is a sum: a starting value plus one number per word that's present.

A sum of "weight × feature" plus a constant is a straight line (or flat plane). Pass it through the S-shaped sigmoid \(1/(1+e^{-z})\) and you get a probability. That's logistic regression's formula.

So NB is secretly a logistic regression whose weights were set by counting. Try the 1-D Gaussian demo: when the two bells have the same width, the log-odds is a straight line. Make the widths different and it bends into a parabola.

With one-hot \(x_{dk}=\mathbb I(x_d=k)\), \(\log[\pi_c\prod_{d,k}\theta_{dck}^{x_{dk}}]=\log\pi_c+\sum_{d,k}x_{dk}\log\theta_{dck}=\boldsymbol\beta_c^\top\mathbf x+\gamma_c\), with \(\beta_{c,dk}=\log\theta_{dck}\), \(\gamma_c=\log\pi_c\). The normalized version is a softmax (Eq. 9.62).

For two classes and binary features, the log-odds is \(w_0+\sum_d w_dx_d\) with \(w_d=\log\frac{\theta_{d1}(1-\theta_{d0})}{\theta_{d0}(1-\theta_{d1})}\). The binary panel computes it and checks that \(\sigma(\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x+w_0)\) matches Eq. 9.47 to the last digit.

The fitting objectives differ. NB maximizes \(\prod_n p(y_n,\mathbf x_n\mid\boldsymbol\theta)\), which decouples into counts. LR maximizes \(\prod_n p(y_n\mid\mathbf x_n,\mathbf w)\), which couples all weights and needs iterative optimization. If the NB assumption holds, both converge to the same classifier. If not, LR's weights are better for prediction.

  • The result holds whenever every \(p(x_d\mid y)\) is in an exponential family with a class-independent base measure and sufficient statistic: \(\log p(x_d\mid c)=\boldsymbol\eta_{dc}^\top T(x_d)-A(\boldsymbol\eta_{dc})+\log h(x_d)\), and \(h\) cancels across classes. For Gaussians, \(T=(x,x^2)\), so LR on features \((x,x^2)\); with tied \(\sigma_d\) the \(x^2\) weight is 0.
  • NB and LR are a generative–discriminative pair (Ng & Jordan 2002): same hypothesis class, different estimators. NB's estimate is the MLE under a constrained joint; LR's is the conditional MLE. Under misspecification, LR is consistent for the best linear classifier in log-loss, NB is not.
  • This is why NB weights can be used to initialize, or regularize toward, LR (e.g. NBSVM, Wang & Manning 2012, which scales features by NB log-count ratios).

Math, slowlyEquations 9.60 – 9.62

One-hot features turn the product into a sum

Let \(x_{dk}=\mathbb I(x_d=k)\). Then

\[ p(\mathbf x\mid y=c,\boldsymbol\theta)=\prod_{d=1}^D\mathrm{Cat}(x_d\mid y=c,\boldsymbol\theta)=\prod_{d=1}^D\prod_{k=1}^K\theta_{dck}^{\,x_{dk}} \tag{9.60} \]
  1. \(\displaystyle p(y=c\mid\mathbf x,\boldsymbol\theta)=\frac{\pi_c\prod_d\prod_k\theta_{dck}^{x_{dk}}}{\sum_{c'}\pi_{c'}\prod_d\prod_k\theta_{dc'k}^{x_{dk}}}\) Eq. 9.47 with 9.60 plugged in.
  2. \(\displaystyle =\frac{\exp\big[\log\pi_c+\sum_d\sum_k x_{dk}\log\theta_{dck}\big]}{\sum_{c'}\exp\big[\log\pi_{c'}+\sum_d\sum_k x_{dk}\log\theta_{dc'k}\big]}\) (9.61) Any positive number equals \(\exp(\log(\cdot))\). The log turns the products into sums and brings the exponent \(x_{dk}\) down front.
  3. Collect the \(\log\theta_{dck}\) into a vector \(\class{tm}{\boldsymbol\beta_c}\) (one entry per \((d,k)\)) and let \(\class{tm}{\gamma_c}=\log\pi_c\). Then the exponent is \(\boldsymbol\beta_c^\top\mathbf x+\gamma_c\). Linear in the one-hot features.
\[ p(y=c\mid\mathbf x,\boldsymbol\theta)=\frac{e^{\class{tm}{\boldsymbol\beta_c}^\top\mathbf x+\class{tm}{\gamma_c}}}{\sum_{c'=1}^Ce^{\boldsymbol\beta_{c'}^\top\mathbf x+\gamma_{c'}}} \tag{9.62} \]

That's the softmax of multinomial logistic regression (§2.5.3).

Two classes, binary features: the sigmoid version

Divide the top and bottom of 9.62 by the ham term and you get \(\sigma(z)\) with \(z\) = (spam exponent) − (ham exponent):

\[ z=\underbrace{\log\frac{\pi_1}{\pi_0}+\sum_d\log\frac{1-\theta_{d1}}{1-\theta_{d0}}}_{w_0}+\sum_d x_d\underbrace{\log\frac{\theta_{d1}(1-\theta_{d0})}{\theta_{d0}(1-\theta_{d1})}}_{w_d} \]

Same form, different objective

\[ \text{NB: }\max_{\boldsymbol\theta}\prod_n p(y_n,\mathbf x_n\mid\boldsymbol\theta)\qquad\qquad\text{LR: }\max_{\mathbf w}\prod_n p(y_n\mid\mathbf x_n,\mathbf w) \]
The joint objective also has to explain \(\mathbf x\), and under the naive assumption it does so badly when features are correlated. The conditional objective spends all its effort on the decision. In general the two give different weights (Exercise 10.3).

§9.4–9.4.1

Generative vs discriminative, and where discriminative wins

A generative classifier models \(p(\mathbf x,y)=p(y)\,p(\mathbf x\mid y)\). It could generate fake examples of each class. A discriminative classifier models only \(p(y\mid\mathbf x)\). It can tell classes apart but can't say what an example looks like. Discriminative models usually win on accuracy, handle arbitrary feature preprocessing, and give better-calibrated probabilities.

Explain itAt your level

Two kids learn to tell cats from dogs.

The first kid learns to draw cats and dogs: ears, tails, whiskers, everything. To decide, she asks "which drawing does this animal look more like?" That's generative.

The second kid only learns the difference: "pointy ears and whiskers means cat." He can't draw either animal, but he's quick and usually right. That's discriminative.

The second kid doesn't waste time learning things that don't help him decide. That's why he's often more accurate. But the first kid is better with only a few pictures to learn from, and she can still decide if part of the animal is hidden.

  • Less to learn. The class-conditional densities can be complicated (bumpy, lots of detail), while the posterior \(p(y\mid x)\) is often a simple S-curve. Figure 9.8's demo shows bumps that change \(p(x\mid y)\) a lot but leave \(p(y\mid x)\) almost unchanged.
  • Feature tricks. You can feed a discriminative model \(x\), \(x^2\), \(x^3\)… or word embeddings, with no need to describe how those features are distributed.
  • Honest probabilities. NB counts correlated clues as if they were separate evidence, so it says "99.9% spam" when it should say "80%." The calibration demo duplicates a word to show this.
  • But: with very little data, NB often wins (the learning-curve demo).

The three advantages in §9.4.1, made concrete:

  • Accuracy. Modeling \(p(\mathbf x\mid y)\) spends parameters and data on structure that may not affect the decision. When the generative model is misspecified (correlated features under NB), its errors propagate into \(p(y\mid\mathbf x)\). LR fits the decision boundary directly and wins asymptotically.
  • Preprocessing. \(p(y\mid\phi(\mathbf x))\) is fine for any \(\phi\). A generative model of \(\phi(\mathbf x)\) has to account for the dependencies \(\phi\) creates (e.g. \(x\) and \(x^2\) are deterministically related).
  • Calibration. If two features are copies, NB counts that evidence twice, so its log-odds is roughly doubled and probabilities are pushed toward 0 or 1.

The learning-curve demo reproduces Ng & Jordan's (2002) finding: NB has higher asymptotic error when its assumption is violated, but reaches its asymptote much faster.

  • Ng & Jordan (2002): for a generative–discriminative pair, \(\epsilon_{\text{LR},\infty}\le\epsilon_{\text{NB},\infty}\), but NB reaches within \(\epsilon\) of its asymptote with \(O(\log D)\) samples versus \(O(D)\) for LR. Hence the crossover. Later work (Xue & Titterington 2008) shows the crossover isn't universal; it depends on the data distribution.
  • Efficiency when correctly specified: Efron (1975) showed that when the Gaussian LDA model is true, LDA is 1.5–3× more statistically efficient than LR. Generative assumptions are information. When they're right they help, and when they're wrong they bias.
  • Calibration: NB's overconfidence is approximately a monotone transform of a decent score, so Platt scaling or isotonic regression on held-out data (Zadrozny & Elkan 2002; Niculescu-Mizil & Caruana 2005) often fixes it while keeping the ranking.
  • Hybrids: train generative models discriminatively (conditional likelihood, maximum margin), or blend objectives (Bouchard & Triggs 2004; Lasserre, Bishop & Minka 2006).

Math, slowlyWhat each approach models

Generative

\[ p(\mathbf x,y)=p(y)\,p(\mathbf x\mid y)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p(y=c\mid\mathbf x)=\frac{p(y=c)\,p(\mathbf x\mid y=c)}{\sum_{c'}p(y=c')\,p(\mathbf x\mid y=c')} \]

Two pieces to fit, then Bayes' rule. You can also sample: draw \(y\sim p(y)\), then \(\mathbf x\sim p(\mathbf x\mid y)\).

Discriminative

\[ p(y\mid\mathbf x)\text{ directly, e.g. } p(y=1\mid\mathbf x)=\sigma(\mathbf w^\top\phi(\mathbf x)+w_0) \]

Nothing is said about how \(\mathbf x\) is distributed, so \(\phi\) can be anything.

Why copies make NB overconfident

  1. If feature \(d\) appears \(k\) times, NB's log-odds gets its vote \(k\) times: \(z_k=\log\frac{\pi_1}{\pi_0}+k\,\log\frac{\theta_{d1}}{\theta_{d0}}+\dots\) Every copy gets its own factor in Eq. 9.46.
  2. The truth: copies are perfectly dependent given the class, \(p(x_d,x_d'\mid c)=p(x_d\mid c)\), so the right log-odds counts the vote once. Seeing the same thing twice isn't new information.
  3. \(\sigma(z_k)\) is pushed toward 0 or 1 as \(k\) grows. The sign of \(z\) often survives, so the label can still be right while the probability is badly off. That's "accurate but poorly calibrated."

The learning-curve demo's ingredients

NB here: class means \(\hat\mu_{dc}\) plus one variance \(\hat\sigma_d^2\) per feature, pooled over both classes and nudged by a weak prior worth 2 pseudo-observations of variance 1 (the Gaussian cousin of §9.3.3's pseudo-counts, so a training set of 8 doesn't produce wild variances). Shared variances give a linear boundary. LR: the conditional MLE with a tiny L2 penalty (λ = 10⁻³, needed when N is smaller than D and the data are separable), fit by Newton's method (IRLS). Both are linear classifiers. Only the fitting objective differs, exactly the §9.3.4 point.

§9.4.2

Where generative classifiers win

Modeling \(\mathbf x\) costs accuracy when data are plentiful, but it buys real things: fitting by counting, handling missing inputs, adding classes without retraining, using unlabeled data, and sometimes robustness to features that only look predictive.

Explain itAt your level

Remember the kid who learned to draw cats and dogs?

  • She learned fast. She just looked at a few of each and took notes.
  • If a bush hides the animal's tail, she still compares what she can see.
  • When a rabbit shows up, she learns to draw rabbits too. Her cat and dog drawings don't change.
  • Even pictures without a name tag help her, because they show her what animals look like.
  • Easy to fit: NB is counting and averaging, one pass over the data. Logistic regression needs an optimizer to run many passes.
  • Missing features: just leave that feature's factor out (§9.4.3).
  • New classes: each class's numbers are computed only from that class's examples. Adding a class means adding a new column of numbers. Nothing else changes. Try removing classes in the digits demo in §9.3.1.
  • Unlabeled data: a generative model can guess the labels of unlabeled examples and learn from them (EM, §8.7).
  • Spurious features: if every cow photo in training happens to be on grass, a discriminative model may learn "grass means cow." A model of what cows look like is less likely to fall for that.
  • Easy to fit: closed-form MLE/MAP from sufficient statistics (§9.3.2). LR is a convex problem needing iterative solvers; neural nets are nonconvex.
  • Missing inputs: marginalize, \(p(y\mid\mathbf x_{\text{obs}})\propto p(y)\sum_{\mathbf x_{\text{mis}}}p(\mathbf x\mid y)\). There is no principled analogue for \(p(y\mid\mathbf x)\).
  • Classes separately: the joint log-likelihood decouples by class (Eq. 9.51), so adding class \(C+1\) touches only \(\boldsymbol\theta_{\cdot,C+1}\) and \(\boldsymbol\pi\). In softmax regression, the normalizer couples all classes.
  • Semi-supervised: \(\log p(\mathcal D_{xy})+\log\sum_y p(\mathcal D_x,y)\), maximized by EM with \(y\) latent for unlabeled points.
  • Spurious features / shift: a model of \(p(\mathbf x\mid y)\) can match the causal direction (label → appearance), which tends to be more stable under distribution shift.
  • Semi-supervised caveats: unlabeled data help only if the generative model is (approximately) correct. Under misspecification, EM can make things worse (Cozman & Cohen 2003). Weighting the unlabeled term (Nigam et al. 2000) is the standard hedge.
  • Causal / anticausal learning (Schölkopf et al. 2012): when \(y\to\mathbf x\) is the causal direction, \(p(\mathbf x\mid y)\) is a stable mechanism, and SSL and generative modeling help; for \(\mathbf x\to y\) problems, \(p(\mathbf x)\) carries no information about \(p(y\mid\mathbf x)\).
  • Open-set detection: \(p(\mathbf x)=\sum_c\pi_cp(\mathbf x\mid c)\) gives an out-of-distribution score for free, though deep generative likelihoods are notoriously unreliable for OOD (Nalisnick et al. 2019).
  • Label shift: if only \(p(y)\) changes at test time, a generative model adapts by re-estimating \(\boldsymbol\pi\) alone (Saerens et al. 2002).

Math, slowlyWhy adding a class is cheap for generative models

From Eq. 9.51 the joint log-likelihood is a sum of independent pieces:

\[ \log p(\mathcal D\mid\boldsymbol\theta)=\log p(\mathcal D_y\mid\boldsymbol\pi)+\sum_{c=1}^{C}\underbrace{\sum_d\log p(\mathcal D_{dc}\mid\theta_{dc})}_{\text{only class }c\text{'s data}} \]

Add class \(C+1\): fit its \(\theta_{d,C+1}\) from its own examples and recompute \(\hat\pi_c=N_c/N\). Every other \(\theta_{dc}\) is unchanged.

Softmax regression's conditional log-likelihood is

\[ \sum_n\Big[\mathbf w_{y_n}^\top\mathbf x_n-\log\sum_{c'}e^{\mathbf w_{c'}^\top\mathbf x_n}\Big] \]

The \(\log\sum_{c'}\) term contains every class's weights for every example. Add a class and the gradient for every \(\mathbf w_c\) changes, so the whole thing has to be re-optimized.

Semi-supervised objective

\[ \log p(\mathcal D\mid\boldsymbol\theta)=\sum_{(\mathbf x,y)\in\mathcal D_{xy}}\log p(\mathbf x,y\mid\boldsymbol\theta)+\sum_{\mathbf x\in\mathcal D_x}\log\sum_{c}p(\mathbf x,y=c\mid\boldsymbol\theta) \]

The second sum is a mixture model with the class as the hidden variable, which is exactly what EM handles. A discriminative \(p(y\mid\mathbf x)\) puts no term on \(\mathbf x\) alone, so unlabeled points don't enter its likelihood at all.

§9.4.3

Handling missing features: under naive Bayes, just skip the factor

If some features aren't observed, a generative classifier sums (marginalizes) them out. Under the naive Bayes assumption the sum of a feature's own factor over all its values is 1, so a missing feature simply drops out of the product. (This assumes the fact that a value is missing says nothing about what it would have been.)

Explain itAt your level

A page of the letter got torn off, so you can't tell if it said "FREE."

Do you pretend it didn't say "FREE"? No, that's a guess, and it might be wrong. The detective just skips that clue and decides using the clues she can still see.

In the digit game, cover the bottom of a number with your hand. The computer only looks at the top part, and it's often still right.

For a missing word, the right move is to average over both possibilities, "it was there" and "it wasn't," weighted by how likely each is:

θ × (rest) + (1 − θ) × (rest) = (θ + 1 − θ) × (rest) = 1 × (rest).

So the missing word's factor becomes 1 and disappears. That's what you see in the table when you set a word to "?".

Filling in a blank with "absent" (0) instead is not the same. It adds a (1 − θ) factor, which is evidence you don't actually have. The occlusion demo shows how that can flip the answer.

Eq. 9.63–9.65: \(p(y=c\mid\mathbf x_{2:D})\propto\pi_c\sum_{x_1}p(x_1,\mathbf x_{2:D}\mid c)\). Under NB the joint factorizes, the sum moves onto the one factor that depends on \(x_1\), and \(\sum_{x_1}p(x_1\mid\theta_{1c})=1\).

For a general generative model you still marginalize, just with more work. For GDA you take the Gaussian's marginal over the observed coordinates (drop rows and columns of \(\boldsymbol\Sigma_c\), §3.2.3).

The assumption doing the heavy lifting: data are missing at random (MAR), i.e. whether a feature is missing doesn't depend on its value. If spammers deliberately hide words, the missingness itself is informative and should be modeled as a feature.

  • Missingness mechanisms (Rubin 1976): MCAR and MAR allow ignoring the mechanism in likelihood-based inference; MNAR does not. Under MNAR, add a missingness indicator \(m_d\) with its own \(p(m_d\mid y)\). With NB that's just one more Bernoulli factor.
  • Training with missing data: NB's MLE simply uses the observed entries for each \((d,c)\) count (\(N_c\) becomes per-feature), which is exact under MAR because the likelihood still factorizes. GDA with missing training entries needs EM.
  • Discriminative workarounds: mean/zero imputation (biased, as the demo shows), multiple imputation, indicator features, or models trained with dropout-style masking. All need a model of \(\mathbf x\) somewhere, or a lot of data with realistic missingness patterns.

Math, slowlyEquations 9.63 – 9.65

Suppose \(x_1\) is missing.

  1. \(\displaystyle p(y=c\mid\mathbf x_{2:D},\boldsymbol\theta)\propto p(y=c\mid\boldsymbol\pi)\,p(\mathbf x_{2:D}\mid y=c,\boldsymbol\theta)\) (9.63) Bayes' rule using only what we observed.
  2. \(\displaystyle =p(y=c\mid\boldsymbol\pi)\sum_{x_1}p(x_1,\mathbf x_{2:D}\mid y=c,\boldsymbol\theta)\) (9.64) The sum rule: the marginal of the observed part is the joint summed over every value the missing part could take.
  3. Under NB, \(\displaystyle\sum_{x_1}p(x_1,\mathbf x_{2:D}\mid c)=\sum_{x_1}\Big[p(x_1\mid\theta_{1c})\prod_{d=2}^Dp(x_d\mid\theta_{dc})\Big]\) Factorize with Eq. 9.46.
  4. \(\displaystyle =\Big[\sum_{x_1}p(x_1\mid\theta_{1c})\Big]\prod_{d=2}^Dp(x_d\mid\theta_{dc})=\prod_{d=2}^Dp(x_d\mid\theta_{dc})\) (9.65) The product over \(d\ge2\) doesn't involve \(x_1\), so it comes out of the sum. What's left is a distribution summed over all its values, which is 1.
For a binary feature the bracket is \(\theta_{1c}+(1-\theta_{1c})=1\). "Fill with 0" instead keeps a factor of \(1-\theta_{1c}\), which differs across classes, and so it changes the answer.
